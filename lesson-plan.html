Where This Fits in Project 2

This lesson is designed as Task 5, Day 1. It teaches the same content as the Pricing Mix Showdown — the four pricing strategies, market conditions that affect pricing power (inflation, recession, supply-chain issues, price elasticity of demand), and the legal constraints on pricing power (price fixing, predatory pricing, price gouging) — through a chocolate theme and interactive web activities. It can replace Day 1 on National Chocolate Day, or run after Day 1 as reinforcement before the Sega Business Case on Day 2. The closing station previews the Day 3 pricing strategy hypothesis work.

CED alignment notes: Topic 2.5.C in the CED lists price fixing (collusion), price gouging and price discrimination based on a protected status, so the Legal Line now includes a price discrimination case. Predatory pricing stays because the Project 2 guide includes it. Cost-based pricing follows CED 2.5.A.5: per-unit cost plus a desired per-unit profit. Price elasticity of demand is explained, not calculated, per the CED exclusion statement. Station 7, AP Exam Practice, uses the CED task verbs and follows the CED sample multiple-choice set and Free-Response Questions 3 and 4.

By the end of this lesson, you will learn:

  • how value-based, competitive, cost-based, and penetration pricing work, and when a business might choose each one;
  • how market conditions — such as supply-chain shortages, inflation, recession, competition, and price elasticity of demand — increase or limit a business's pricing power;
  • which legal constraints (laws against price fixing, price gouging, price discrimination and predatory pricing) limit how businesses set prices;
  • how to use AP task verbs to answer exam-style multiple-choice and free-response questions about pricing;
  • how to write a pricing strategy hypothesis for your Business Canvas Project.

Lesson Procedure

Warm Up (5 min)

  1. Project the Warm Up slide. Students open index.html, enter their name, and choose a bar.
  2. Students type which customer characteristic drove their choice (price sensitivity, status, convenience, values, health, brand loyalty).
  3. Flip the three fact cards together. Ask: "If cocoa prices spiked, which of these three brands could raise its price most easily? Why?"

Pricing Strategy Sort (7 min)

  1. Use the strategies slide to define value-based, competitive, cost-based, and penetration pricing.
  2. Pairs match the four products, then check. Discuss any misses.

The Cocoa Crunch (8 min)

  1. Define pricing power and price elasticity of demand (elastic = buyers react strongly to price; inelastic = they keep buying).
  2. Students work through six headlines. In whole-class mode, use a quick thumbs up (increases) / thumbs down (limits) before clicking.
  3. Students answer the shrinkflation prompt in writing.

Price Your Bar Lab (15 min)

  1. Explain the setup: the competitor charges $3.00; projected sales depend on price compared with $3.00 and on the segment's price sensitivity.
  2. Students test at least three prices and two segments, then settle on a strategy and price. The strategy check gives feedback on whether the price matches the strategy.
  3. Trigger the Cocoa Shock. Students choose a response (raise price, shrink the bar, cut costs, or accept a smaller margin) and write a justification.
Discussion question: "Cost-based pricing gave you a 'correct' price — but did customers buy it? What does that tell you about using cost alone?"

The Legal Line (5 min)

  1. Students rule on four cases and check their answers. Briefly discuss why the Valentine's price increase is legal.

Exit Ticket (5 min) and Connect (3 min)

  1. Students complete the exit ticket individually.
  2. Students draft a pricing strategy hypothesis for their Business Canvas Project using the sentence stem, then click Build my work summary and copy it into the LMS assignment or print it.

AP Exam Practice (25 min, next class or homework)

  1. Students review the six CED task verbs. Point out that Identify needs no explanation, while Explain needs a "because" or "which leads to" link to evidence.
  2. Students answer multiple-choice questions 1–4 (a stimulus set like the CED sample questions), then check them.
  3. Students write each free-response part before opening its scoring guidelines, then self-score. Self-scores and responses appear in the work summary.
  4. Debrief: share one response that earned FRQ 4(C) and one that didn't. Why did it miss? The usual causes are no evidence for one option or "it depends."

AP Practice: Free-Response Scoring Guidelines

Modeled on the CED sample scoring guidelines. Accept other responses that meet the task verb with accurate evidence from the scenario.

Business Concept Application (A) Describe a market research method Peak Cocoa used to gather the information in the scenario.

1 point

  • Peak Cocoa conducted primary research by running an in-store price test, selling the bar at three different prices in three similar stores and recording weekly sales.
  • Peak Cocoa ran an experiment that tested how customers responded to different prices.
  • Peak Cocoa used secondary research when it reviewed an industry report on cocoa costs.

Does not earn credit: Naming a method with no detail from the scenario (for example, “they did research”), or naming a method that doesn't appear in the scenario, such as a focus group.

Business Concept Application (B) Describe a finding revealed by the research.

1 point

  • At $2.99, the bar generated the highest weekly revenue, $1,614.60.
  • When the price rose from $2.99 to $3.49, weekly sales fell from 540 to 300 bars, nearly cutting sales in half.
  • Raising the price from $2.49 to $2.99 reduced sales only slightly, from 600 to 540 bars, but increased revenue.
  • Cocoa costs rose sharply over the past year, according to the industry report.

Does not earn credit: A finding with no data or detail from the scenario (for example, “price matters to customers”).

Business Concept Application (C) Explain how Peak Cocoa could use a finding from the research to achieve a business goal.

1 point

  • Because $2.99 produced the most revenue and is still within rivals' $2.29–$2.99 range, Peak Cocoa could use competitive pricing at $2.99 to increase weekly revenue without losing customers to rivals.
  • Sales dropped sharply at $3.49, which shows customers are highly price sensitive above $2.99. Peak Cocoa should avoid that price, because it would push customers to rival bars and lower revenue and market share.
  • Because cocoa costs are rising but customers respond strongly to price, Peak Cocoa has limited pricing power. It could raise its price only to $2.99 and look for other ways to cut per-unit costs to protect profit.

Does not earn credit: Restating a finding without connecting it to a goal, or naming a goal without explaining how the finding helps reach it (no “because” or “which leads to” link).

Business Decision (practice) (A) Like all businesses, B&B is affected by market and external factors that have an impact on its ability to achieve its goals.
i. Describe a market or an external factor indicated in the scenario that affects B&B.
ii. Explain how the factor you selected in part A(i) creates an opportunity or a problem for B&B.

i. Describe a factor (1 point)

  • Poor cocoa harvests in West Africa (an external, supply-chain factor) raised B&B's per-unit cost from $2.10 to $2.80.
  • B&B's customers are attentive shoppers: 62% check package size.

ii. Explain how it creates an opportunity or a problem (1 point)

  • The cocoa shortage is a problem because it cuts B&B's per-unit profit from $1.40 to $0.70 at the current price. B&B has to raise its price, reduce its costs or accept much lower profit.
  • Because so many customers check package size, shrinking the bar is a problem. Customers are likely to notice, which could hurt the trust B&B's brand depends on.

Does not earn credit: Naming a factor without scenario evidence, or describing the factor again in part ii without explaining its effect on B&B.

Business Decision (practice) (B) There are financial and nonfinancial criteria that can be used to compare the two options.
i. Using projected weekly profit as a criterion, describe a difference or similarity between the two options. Include specific evidence related to each option.
ii. Using one nonfinancial criterion relevant to the decision, describe a difference or similarity between the two options. Include specific evidence related to each option.

i. Financial criterion (1 point)

  • Option 2 has a higher projected weekly profit ($997.50) than Option 1 ($952.00), a difference of $45.50 per week.
  • Both options are projected to stay profitable, earning $952.00 and $997.50 per week before fixed costs.

ii. Nonfinancial criterion (1 point)

  • Customer trust and brand reputation: Option 1 keeps B&B's honest-labeling promise by openly raising the price. Option 2 risks the 70% of customers who would feel misled by a smaller bar.
  • Customer loyalty: under Option 1, some price-sensitive customers may leave (sales are projected to drop to 800). Under Option 2, loyal customers who notice the smaller bar may stop recommending B&B online.

Does not earn credit: A comparison that uses evidence for only one option, or a nonfinancial criterion that isn't connected to the scenario.

Business Decision (practice) (C) Recommend a course of action for B&B. In your response, you should do the following:
• Recommend one of the two options in the scenario.
• Explain why you recommend that option.
• Support your recommendation using two specific criteria, which may include the criteria from part B. Include evidence from the scenario related to each criterion you use.

Recommend and explain (1 point)

  • I recommend Option 1. Raising the price openly protects the honest brand that B&B's loyal customers value, which matters more in the long run than a small short-term profit difference.
  • I recommend Option 2. It earns more profit each week while keeping the price customers are used to, which protects B&B's market share.

Support with two criteria and evidence (1 point)

  • Option 1: Although it earns $45.50 less per week ($952.00 vs. $997.50), it avoids misleading the 70% of customers who would feel misled by a smaller bar. That protects the trust and online recommendations B&B's brand depends on.
  • Option 2: It earns $997.50 per week instead of $952.00, and keeping the $3.50 price avoids losing about 200 price-sensitive customers each week. B&B could reduce trust risk by clearly labeling the new size.

Does not earn credit: Recommending both options or neither (“it depends”), or a recommendation without evidence from the scenario for each criterion.

Substitute & Self-Paced Plan

No business background needed. The lesson runs itself: every station includes blue "On your own" directions, instant feedback, and explanations.

  1. Write the lesson link on the board (your GitHub Pages address for index.html).
  2. Students open the link, type their name at the top, and work through Stations 1–6 and 8 in order (Station 7 is next class or homework unless your teacher says otherwise). Suggested times appear on each station; the timer in the bottom corner is optional.
  3. Students may work with a partner for Stations 2–4 but must complete the Exit Ticket on their own.
  4. At the end, students go to Station 7, click Build my work summary, then Copy and paste it into the class assignment (or Print / save as PDF).
  5. If students get stuck, have them open the 🎬 explainer video in that station before asking for help.
  6. Students who finish early can start Station 7, AP Exam Practice, or redo the Lab with a different customer segment.

Troubleshooting: Answers are saved in the student's browser as they work. If a student switches devices, answers will not follow them — have them finish on one device. If the copy button doesn't work, students can select the text in the summary box and copy it manually. Students without a device can use the printable exit ticket on the next page.
